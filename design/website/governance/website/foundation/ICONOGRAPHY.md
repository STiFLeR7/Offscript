# ICONOGRAPHY — The Design Discipline of the Mark That Means

> **What this is.** The Foundation discipline that owns **the small reduced mark and what it is for.**
> It decides when a mark earns a place, what a mark may be asked to do, and how far a form may be
> reduced while still meaning what it meant. Its output is not an icon, a set or a file; it is
> **iconic intent**.
>
> **What this is not.** Not illustration — a creative that carries content belongs to Illustration, and
> the boundary is scale of *responsibility*, not scale in pixels. Not typography, though both are
> reduced marks carrying meaning; type is a system for encoding language and this discipline governs
> marks that stand outside it. Not colour, not spacing, not layout. It names no library and no form.
>
> **The one boundary above all others.** *Iconography determines when a reduced mark is warranted and
> what it is asked to do. It never decides what a mark looks like in specific, never carries content
> that words are carrying, and never decorates. It governs iconic intent, and nothing else.*
>
> **The test every line passes.** *Would this still be true if the icon set were replaced entirely,
> while marks still did the same work?*
>
> **Where this sits.** A Design Discipline of the Website Foundation, reasoned through after
> `EDITORIAL_ART_DIRECTION.md` — which tells it whether a moment wants marking at all. **On any
> conflict, Governance wins, and this document wins over `rulebooks/visual-language.md` §9, which
> operationalizes it.**

---

## §0 — First principles

A reduced mark is the smallest thing in an interface that carries meaning without spelling it. It is
read faster than a word and remembered longer, and it works by a trade the reader makes without
noticing: they accept an abstraction in exchange for speed.

That trade is the discipline's entire subject, and it fails in two opposite directions. Reduce too
little and the mark is a small picture — slower to read than the word it replaced, and carrying detail
that must be resolved before it can be understood. Reduce too far and it is a shape the reader must be
taught, at which point it has stopped being a shortcut and become a second vocabulary to learn.

Between those failures is a narrow band where a mark is instantly legible and instantly meaningful,
and the discipline exists to find it.

But there is a prior question that most icon governance never asks. Systems reliably govern what an
icon should *look* like — one set, one weight, one style — and reliably never govern whether an icon
should be *there*. That asymmetry has a predictable outcome: rules that only constrain form can be
satisfied perfectly by a page with no icons at all, and by a page covered in them, provided all of
them match.

So a system with excellent icon hygiene and no rule about occasion produces one of two failures and
cannot tell which it has. Either marks appear everywhere as visual seasoning, or they disappear
entirely and the interface loses the fastest instrument it had for orienting a reader. Both look
compliant. Neither is governed.

## §1 — Identity

Iconography is the discipline of **the mark that means**.

It owns two questions that are usually collapsed into one: *should there be a mark here*, and *what is
the mark being asked to do*. Form follows from both, and a discipline that governs form without them
is governing the least consequential part of its own subject.

It deserves independent ownership because a reduced mark is not a small illustration and not a glyph.
It is its own thing — an abstraction traded for recognition speed — and neither neighbouring discipline
reasons about that trade.

## §2 — Mission

Iconography exists so that **a reader is oriented faster than reading would allow, and never asked to
interpret a mark that is doing nothing.**

Success is an interface where every mark is earning its place, where a reader could say what each one
is for, and where their absence in the places without them is as deliberate as their presence in the
places with them.

## §3 — Constitutional Responsibility

This discipline owns **iconic intent**: whether a moment warrants a mark, what the mark is for, and
how far reduction may go.

**What it inherits.** Whether a moment wants marking, from Editorial Art Direction. What must be
perceptible, from Visual Language. What information exists, from Information Architecture — a mark
never introduces meaning that was not already there. The palette, from Colour, where a mark carries
an accent.

**What it contributes.** The occasions on which a mark is warranted, the roles a mark may hold, and
the reduction discipline that keeps a mark legible.

**What it rejects.** It does not carry content that words are carrying. It does not decorate — a mark
placed for visual interest is outside this discipline entirely, not a lenient case within it. It does
not choose the accent a mark takes. It does not govern a creative that carries content; that is
Illustration.

## §4 — Core Question

> **What is this mark doing that the words are not already doing?**

If the answer is *repeating them*, the mark is decoration wearing a function. If the answer is *saying
something the words do not*, the mark is carrying content and belongs to a discipline that governs
content. The narrow correct answer is that it is doing the same work **faster**, or marking a
structure the words cannot mark.

## §5 — Inputs

- **The felt register** (Editorial Art Direction) — whether the moment wants marking, and how firmly.
- **The Information Model** — what exists to be marked. A mark never adds meaning.
- **The Visual Model** — what must be perceptible and in what order.
- **The Mechanism Model** — the structures a mark might punctuate.
- **The accent hierarchy** (Colour) — where a mark may carry colour.

## §6 — Transformation

> **A moment that may want marking → a mark with a job, or no mark.**

What enters is a moment with a structure and a register. What is applied is the judgment of whether
speed or orientation is genuinely gained by a mark here. What emerges is iconic intent — frequently
the decision that no mark is warranted, which is a decision and not an omission.

## §7 — Outputs

- **The occasions** — the circumstances under which a mark is warranted.
- **The roles** — what a mark may be asked to do.
- **The reduction discipline** — how far a form may be simplified and still mean.

It outputs no icon, no set and no form.

## §8 — Discipline Knowledge

### A mark is a trade of precision for speed

The reader accepts a less precise signal in exchange for a faster one. This frames every decision in
the discipline: a mark is worth having only where the speed gained exceeds the precision lost. A mark
beside a word that was already unambiguous gained no speed — the word was already read — and lost
whatever attention the mark took.

### Recognition is learned, not intrinsic

Very few marks are universally understood. Most are conventions a reader has encountered before, and
their legibility is a fact about the reader's history rather than about the form. A system inventing
its own marks is asking readers to learn a vocabulary, and the cost of that is paid on first
encounter, by everyone, forever.

This is why convention is worth more than originality here, and why the discipline's instinct should
be to reach for the expected mark rather than the distinctive one. Distinctiveness in this discipline
is bought with comprehension.

### Reduction has a floor and a ceiling

Below the floor, a mark is a picture: it carries detail that must be resolved, and it reads slower
than the word. Above the ceiling, it is a shape: unambiguous only to someone who has been told. The
usable band is narrow and is a property of the *concept*, not of the system — some ideas reduce
cleanly and some do not, and a concept that will not reduce should not be given a mark at all.

The most common error is treating reduction as a style question, applying one degree uniformly. It is
a legibility question, and the answer varies by what is being marked.

### Consistency of form is hygiene; occasion is the discipline

A single set, a single weight, a single degree of reduction — these matter, and they are the easy
part. They make marks read as one family, and their absence is immediately visible.

But form consistency is entirely satisfiable by an interface with no marks, and by one saturated with
them. **A rule that cannot distinguish those two states is not governing iconography.** The
consequential question is occasion, and it is the one most systems never write down — which is why so
many well-governed interfaces have icons that are perfectly consistent and completely arbitrary.

### The roles a mark can hold are few

A mark is doing one of a small number of things: opening something so it is found; anchoring a
structure so it can be returned to; identifying a category so it is recognized across the experience;
marking a transition; or reinforcing a state the words also carry.

Naming these matters because it makes the occasion question answerable. *Should there be a mark here?*
is a matter of taste. *Which of these jobs is it doing?* has an answer, and "none of them" is the most
useful answer the discipline produces.

### A mark that repeats its label is decoration

The most common decorative pattern is also the least recognized, because it looks purposeful: a mark
placed beside a word that says the same thing. It adds no speed — the word is right there — and it
costs attention. This is decoration with a justification attached, and it survives review precisely
because the justification sounds like a function.

### Scarcity is per occasion, not per page

Marks work by standing out from what surrounds them — but *what surrounds them* is the occasion, not
the document. The rule that matters is **one mark per role**: a role carrying two marks has made
neither findable, and that is the crowding the instrument actually suffers from. A page on which
twenty distinct roles each carry one mark is not crowded; it is legible.

This is narrowed from what stood here before, which read scarcity as a property of the page and
counselled restraint in the aggregate. That formulation could not distinguish the two very different
things it was condemning — a feature list where every item repeats the same generic glyph, and a page
where navigation, process steps, metrics and status each carry the one mark that belongs to them. The
first is the failure; the second is the system working. *(`DECISION-REGISTER.md` DR-8.)*

### Where semantic meaning exists, prefer icon and text over text alone

The active form of Occasion, and the half this discipline was missing. A mark is not merely permitted
where a role warrants one — it is **reached for**. Repetitive text patterns are the standing
opportunity: a list of eight capabilities, a four-step process, a row of metrics, a set of status
values. Each of those carries semantic structure that a reader currently has to extract from prose,
and a mark makes it scannable at a glance.

The instruction is directional and deliberately so: *look for the opportunity*. A system that waits to
be told where a mark belongs will place none, which is the measured history here — see Artifact B.

**The decoration bans are untouched and now carry the whole weight.** A mark that repeats its label is
still decoration. A mark that means nothing is still decoration. Widening the occasion does not widen
what a mark may be; it widens where the question is asked. Every mark still has to answer *which job
is it doing?*, and "none of them" is still the most useful answer this discipline produces.

## §9 — Organizational Design System Decisions

- **One set, and never two.** Adopted because marks from different systems read as different voices,
  and the inconsistency is legible even to readers who cannot name it.
- **One stroke weight, scaling with size rather than holding constant.** Adopted so marks read as one
  family at every scale.
- **A mark is reached for by role, and a mark with no role does not appear.** Adopted because this is
  the rule that makes the discipline governable, and because form rules alone cannot distinguish an
  interface with no marks from one covered in them.
- **The roles are enumerated, and the enumeration is what causes marks to appear.** Adopted 2026-07-29
  because the rule above, alone, produced no marks at all — it says when a mark may *not* appear and
  never when it must. The governed occasions are: feature lists · process · timeline · cards ·
  metrics · tabs · callouts · labels · CTA supporting text · navigation · footer · information
  blocks · problem statements · benefits · status · checklists · quote attribution · testimonial
  metadata · contact blocks · industries · services · AI capabilities · section labels · value
  propositions · empty states · floating contextual markers. A role on this list carries its mark;
  a role not on it still has to justify one.
- **Iconography is measured by distribution, not by presence.** Adopted because a page with one mark
  in one section satisfies every rule above and is not a system — it is an ornament. Coverage is
  assessed across the eligible roles the page actually contains, with purely typographic and
  media-only sections excluded from the reckoning rather than counted as failures.
- **A mark never carries meaning alone.** Adopted as an accessibility floor: a reader who does not
  recognize a mark must lose nothing.
- **The filled chip is a defined form with its own occasion**, not an exception to the outline rule.
  **The tile is a filled surface; the glyph inside it stays outline.** *(`DECISION-REGISTER.md` DR-3,
  resolved 2026-07-29. The direction sanctions the form — orange serves "floating icons" and "corner
  accents" — while holding "avoid filled icons" for the mark itself. Those read as contradictory only
  until the chip and the glyph are separated, which is what the reserved category was always for.)*
  Its occasion is the **floating contextual marker**: a chip appears where a mark must attach to a
  composition rather than to a line of text — overlapping a creative's edge, anchoring a corner. A
  chip inside running text is a filled icon, which is still refused.

## §10 — Primary Primitives

**Signification — what the mark means.** The relationship between a form and the idea it stands for.
Signification is inherited rather than invented: a mark means what readers have learned it means, and
a system that decides otherwise is not signifying but teaching.

**Reduction — how far the form is stripped.** The primitive that governs legibility. Reduction has a
floor and a ceiling and a usable band between them, and the band is a property of the concept rather
than of the system.

**Occasion — whether a mark is warranted at all.** The primitive that governs presence, and the one
most systems omit. Occasion is what separates a governed iconography from a consistent one, and
without it the other two primitives describe marks that had no business existing.

The three are ordered: Occasion decides whether there is a mark, Signification decides what it must
convey, Reduction decides how far it may be simplified while still conveying it. Reasoning in any
other order produces a well-made mark that should not be there.

## §11 — Decision Intelligence

**Ask whether the mark is warranted before asking what it should be.** Form is the last question. A
process that starts with form will find a form, and the mark will exist because the process produced
one.

**Name the role, or do not place the mark.** If none of the available roles fits, the honest answer is
that no mark is warranted. "It looks better with one" is a role this discipline does not have.

**Reach for the conventional mark.** Recognition is learned, so the expected form is worth more than
the distinctive one. Originality here is bought with comprehension, at the reader's expense.

**Test reduction against the concept, not against the set.** Some ideas reduce cleanly and some do
not. Applying one degree uniformly produces marks that are stylistically consistent and individually
illegible.

**Treat repetition of a nearby label as decoration.** A mark beside a word that says the same thing
gained no speed. This is the most common decorative pattern and the one most likely to survive review.

**Protect scarcity actively.** Each additional mark reduces the effectiveness of every existing one.
The relevant question is never whether *this* mark is defensible but whether the interface can still
afford one.

**Let the register decide whether the moment wants marking at all.** A quiet moment may want no
punctuation, and adding it because the structure permits one is how a page becomes uniformly busy.

## §12 — Behaviour

The discipline behaves like an editor working with punctuation: aware that the marks are not the
writing, precise about placement, and reluctant to add. It is conventional by choice, treating
familiarity as a feature. It is comfortable concluding that no mark is needed, and it treats that
conclusion as the discipline working rather than as it declining to contribute.

## §13 — Cross-Discipline Relationships

- **Editorial Art Direction, consulted before.** Says whether a moment wants marking. This discipline
  never re-decides register.
- **Illustration.** The seam is responsibility, not size: a creative *carries* content, a mark
  *points* at content that already exists. Where a mark begins carrying meaning the words do not, it
  has become a creative and belongs to Illustration.
- **Typography.** Both are reduced marks carrying meaning. Type encodes language; this discipline
  governs marks outside it. Where a mark substitutes for a word within running text, Typography
  governs.
- **Colour.** Decides whether a mark may carry an accent and which. This discipline decides the form
  it takes.
- **Spacing.** Owns the intervals around a mark. This discipline never sets one.
- **Information Architecture, above.** Supplies what exists. A mark never introduces meaning.
- **`rulebooks/visual-language.md` §9, below.** Operationalizes this expertise into the set, the
  weight and the permitted forms. It inherits this reasoning and never authors it.

## §14 — Quality Intelligence

- What is this mark doing that the words are not already doing?
- Which role is it holding? If none, why is it here?
- Would a reader unfamiliar with this mark lose anything?
- Is this the conventional form, and if not, what was gained by departing from it?
- Does this concept reduce cleanly, or has it been forced into a shape?
- How many marks does this moment carry, and can the interface still afford them?
- Is there any place where a mark would genuinely have helped and none appears?

## §15 — Failure Modes

**Form governed, occasion ungoverned.** Rules that constrain appearance and never presence. Satisfied
equally by an interface with no marks and one saturated with them — the discipline's defining failure,
and invisible to any check that only reads form.

**Label repetition.** A mark beside a word that says the same thing. Decoration with a justification
attached.

**Invented signification.** A bespoke mark for a concept with a conventional one. Comprehension traded
for distinctiveness, paid on first encounter by everyone.

**Uniform reduction.** One degree applied to every concept. Stylistically consistent, individually
illegible for whichever concepts did not fit.

**Saturation.** Marks so frequent they become the baseline. The instrument still present and no longer
able to direct anything.

**Absence by omission.** No marks anywhere, because no rule ever produced one. Perfect hygiene, and
the fastest orientation instrument unused.

**Mark as sole signal.** Meaning carried by a form alone, lost on any reader who does not recognize
it.

## §16 — Verification

**Occasion.** Every mark holds a named role — checkable, and checked.

**Presence.** Marks appear where the discipline requires them. A page with none must be able to fail
this, or only form is governed.

**Family.** One set, one weight, consistent reduction.

**Independence.** No meaning is carried by a mark alone.

**Scarcity.** Mark density per moment stays within bounds.

## §17 — Evolution

The discipline matures by getting better at *not* placing marks, and by understanding recognition more
deeply — not by acquiring more of them. A larger set is a weaker one: it offers a mark for every
concept, and the availability of a mark is not a reason to use it.

It grows by learning which marks readers actually recognize, by getting more precise about occasion,
and by finding roles that were always one role. It does not grow by following icon-style trends, which
change what a mark looks like and nothing about whether it should be there.
