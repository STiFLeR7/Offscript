# value-stats

Proof-forward value prop; numbers carry the argument.

## Purpose

Makes the value argument with hard numbers in the foreground, so the case is carried by evidence
rather than description. It solves the unbacked-claim problem: when the most convincing thing the
page can say is a measured result, leading with the figures lets proof do the persuading instead
of adjectives.

## Choose when

- Real, checkable numbers exist and are the most persuasive thing the section can say.
- The audience is evaluating and responds to evidence more than to description.
- The value is better demonstrated by a measured outcome than explained in prose.
- The section's job is to convert interest into belief with proof in hand.

## Avoid when

- There are no genuine numbers — reach for a descriptive feature band rather than invent figures.
- The argument is qualitative, about how something feels or works — numbers would misrepresent it.
- The point is purely the figures with no value framing around them — reach for a dedicated
  stats / outcomes band where the numerals themselves are the whole argument.

## Character

A confident, proof-forward band of medium density where the numerals are the focal point and the
copy frames them. The energy is assured and credible rather than decorative, and it is read as
evidence, not operated.

## Composition

It follows the promise as the proof beat that backs it, sitting comfortably near outcomes or stats
bands and handing off toward a close. It should not stack directly against another dense
number-heavy band, where two proof beats blur into one.

## Contract

Assumes real, verifiable numbers exist and can be stated plainly, with enough value context to
make them meaningful. It expects the figures to be honest and current — the section's credibility
rests entirely on them.

## Judgement

Used well, a measured result anchors the value claim and makes it believable on sight. The
tempting misuse is reaching for it when the numbers are soft, invented, or stale, or when the
argument was never quantitative. An unbacked or inflated figure does not just fail to persuade —
it spends the trust every honest claim on the page depends on.
