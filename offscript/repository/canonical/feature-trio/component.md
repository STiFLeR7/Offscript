# feature-trio

Three peer items at a glance.

## Purpose

Presents a small set of capabilities as genuine equals, each given the same weight, so the reader
takes in three co-ordinate reasons to care in one glance. It solves the opposite problem to a
dominance layout: when items are true peers, any emphasis would misstate their importance, so
parity itself is the message.

## Choose when

- There are about three capabilities that genuinely matter equally and none should lead.
- The audience is scanning, early-to-mid journey, to grasp the breadth of what is offered.
- The argument is "here are the few things this does well", stated without ranking.
- A balanced, legible overview serves the page better than a single hero capability.

## Avoid when

- One feature is really the headline and the others support it — reach for a dominance-led
  feature band so the lead can win.
- The items are actually an ordered sequence — reach for a stepped or how-it-works treatment.
- There are far more than a few peers — a denser grid or an opt-in list carries that load better.

## Character

A calm, balanced band of medium-low density and even emphasis: three equal regions with no single
focal point by design. The energy is steady and scannable rather than directed, and it is read at
a glance, not operated.

## Composition

It usually follows the hero as the first plain expansion of the promise, and hands off to deeper
feature detail, process, or proof. Because its evenness is the point, it is used once on a page;
two equal-card grids back to back flatten both into visual monotony.

## Contract

Assumes three (or close to three) capabilities of genuinely comparable importance, each
expressible in a short, parallel claim. It expects real peers — not a ranked set padded out to
three, and not one strong item carried by two weak ones.

## Judgement

Used well, a product shows three co-equal strengths a reader can absorb at once. The tempting
misuse is forcing a ranked set into equal cells, so an item that should lead is quietly demoted —
or padding to three when only two are real. When the peers are not truly equal, the parity reads
as a missed hierarchy and the strongest point loses its due weight.
