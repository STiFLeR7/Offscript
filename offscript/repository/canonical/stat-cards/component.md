# stat-cards

A strong contrasting mid-page proof beat.

## Purpose

Lands the page's hardest proof as a strong, contrasting beat of large numbers a reader cannot miss.
It solves the unbelieved-claim problem at its most demanding point: when the argument needs a jolt
of credibility mid-page, a high-contrast set of headline figures answers doubt with evidence that
is checkable on sight, not merely asserted.

## Choose when

- The most persuasive thing the page can say at this point is a small set of bold, checkable
  numbers.
- The argument needs a deliberate change of register — a punchy proof beat that stands apart from
  the calmer bands around it.
- The reader is mid-journey and weighing credibility, ready to be convinced by figures.
- A few metrics are strong enough to carry the section on their own.

## Avoid when

- The numbers are soft, vague, or not genuinely impressive — a loud treatment only magnifies a
  weak figure; reach for a quieter proof row instead.
- Proof is better delivered by a human voice — reach for a testimonials role.
- It would sit directly beside another high-contrast band, where two punches blunt each other (the
  reason its own rule forbids that adjacency).

## Character

A bold, high-contrast band of concentrated density and confident energy: a deliberate visual jolt
where a few large figures are the whole focus. It is the family's loudest proof moment, read as
evidence rather than operated, and built to stand out from its neighbours.

## Composition

It belongs mid-page, as the contrasting proof punch after a claim has been made, with calmer bands
on either side to let it register. It must not sit next to another contrasting band, and it needs
rest around it — two heavy proof beats in a row cancel each other.

## Contract

Assumes a few real, current, verifiable numbers strong enough to justify a loud treatment, each
stated specifically rather than rounded to impress. It expects genuinely headline figures — the
contrast promises significance the numbers must repay.

## Judgement

Used well, a mid-page doubt is answered by two or three bold figures the reader can check. The
tempting misuse is reaching for the loud treatment when the numbers are ordinary, or stacking it
against another contrast band. When the figures do not earn the volume, the contrast reads as
overstatement and the proof works against itself.
