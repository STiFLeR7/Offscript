# logo-strip

Early trust marks under the hero; marks only.

## Purpose

The early trust strip: a single quiet row of recognisable client, partner, or investor marks placed
directly beneath the hero. It does one thing — signal, before the reader has been given a single
reason, that credible others already rely on this — and in doing so buys the attention the rest of the
page still has to earn.

## Choose when

- Borrowed credibility belongs at the very start, before any argument has been made.
- The names on offer are recognisable enough that a glance is all it takes.
- The hero has promised something and a light, immediate "serious people trust this" keeps the reader
  moving.
- Marks alone carry the signal; no story or number is needed here.

## Avoid when

- The marks would need explaining — recognition, not reading, is the whole mechanism.
- One customer story should anchor a few numbers — reach for the proof-hybrid shape.
- A heavier, deliberately assembled wall of backers is what the page wants — reach for that shape.
- The proof is a voice — reach for a testimonials role — or a bare number — reach for a stats /
  outcomes role.

## Character

Quiet, ambient, and low-effort: a thin band of marks meant to be glanced at, not studied. It is the
lightest member of its family — recognition does the work in an instant, and it never raises its voice
or asks for anything. Its whole value is being noticed without being dwelt on.

## Composition

It sits high, immediately under the hero and before the first real argument, and hands the reader
onward with a little more patience than they arrived with. It should stay a single uncluttered row,
never swell into a focal feature, and never crowd the hero it exists to support.

## Contract

Assumes a handful of real, permitted marks recognisable to this audience. It expects every logo to be
genuine and every implied relationship to be true; a borrowed or invented mark, or a row so crowded
that nothing registers, breaks the instant recognition that is its only job.

## Judgement

Used well, a reader arrives sceptical and, in one glance, is given a low-effort reason to keep reading.
The tempting misuse is padding the row with unrecognisable or unearned marks, or letting the strip grow
into the page's focal point. Its power is entirely borrowed — it supports the argument, it is never the
argument, and a single fabricated mark forfeits all of it.
