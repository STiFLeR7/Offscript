# testimonial-slider

One long voice at a time.

## Purpose

The one-at-a-time shape: a longer single voice given the whole stage, with others waiting a step away.
It answers the "these quotes are too clipped to trust" problem by letting each testimony run at length,
one at a time, so a substantial quote can breathe without a wall of text competing beside it.

## Choose when

- Each voice is long enough to deserve the full stage rather than a shared grid.
- Several substantial quotes exist and reading them one at a time suits the pace.
- The reader will engage — willing to move through voices rather than scan them all at once.
- Depth per quote matters more than seeing every voice simultaneously.

## Avoid when

- The quotes are short and the point is their number — reach for a review carousel.
- Breadth seen at a glance is the argument — reach for a multi-voice set.
- One story alone carries it, or needs true narrative depth — reach for a single card or a deep story.
- The proof is a mark — reach for a social-proof role — or a number — reach for a stats / outcomes
  role.

## Character

Measured and unhurried: one substantial voice at a time, given room to be read in full. It is the
family's interactive shape — advanced one testimony at a time — but the motion serves legibility,
letting each long quote hold the stage rather than adding spectacle.

## Composition

It sits in the body beside the claims its voices support, and reads well where the page can pause on
proof. It should not sit beside another testimonials band, nor hide essential proof behind interaction
the reader may never advance — the first voice must stand on its own.

## Contract

Assumes several real, permitted, and genuinely substantial quotes with attribution. It expects each
voice to be real and long enough to justify the one-at-a-time treatment; thin quotes stretched into a
slider waste the format, and a fabricated voice collapses the proof.

## Judgement

Used well, each of a few substantial customers gets the stage to say, at length, that this delivered.
The tempting misuse is stepping through clipped one-liners that a simple set would show better, or
burying the only real proof behind a control the reader never touches. Padded with thin quotes it
feels like filler; if the resting voice is weak, the proof never lands.
